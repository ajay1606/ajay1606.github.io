Project Overview

This project develops an autonomous mine-rescue and inspection robot using the Boston Dynamics SPOT quadruped platform. The objective is to enable a robotic system to independently navigate underground environments, build maps, avoid obstacles, detect people, and provide situational information that can assist rescue and mine-safety operations.

SPOT is augmented with mounted LiDAR and camera sensors and an autonomous navigation software stack integrating sensing, localization, perception, map-based planning, motion planning, and robot control. The system is designed to operate in complex underground spaces where GPS is unavailable and where irregular geometry, narrow passages, poor visibility, and environmental hazards make conventional robotic navigation difficult.

A complementary thermal-perception module uses YOLOv11 trained on thermal imagery to detect people and support situational awareness when conventional visible-light sensing may be limited. The complete system was evaluated in the Missouri University of Science and Technology Experimental Mine.

Motivation: Robotics for Mine Rescue

Underground rescue operations can expose personnel to unstable structures, smoke, darkness, restricted passages, hazardous gases, and uncertain environmental conditions. Quickly understanding the state of the mine is critical, yet sending human responders into an unknown area can introduce substantial risk.

A mobile robotic platform can act as an initial autonomous scout by entering the environment, generating maps, identifying traversable routes, detecting people, and returning environmental information to rescue personnel before human entry.

Research objective: develop an autonomous robotic system capable of navigating GPS-denied underground environments while providing spatial mapping and perception information for safer and more informed rescue operations.

Autonomous Navigation Architecture

The SPOT autonomy stack is organized around sensing, localization, perception, planning, and robot control. LiDAR and camera measurements provide environmental observations, while previously generated map information supports localization and route planning.

1

Sensing

LiDAR and camera sensors continuously observe the surrounding underground environment.

2

Localization

Sensor observations are aligned with map information to estimate the robot's current position and motion.

3

Perception

Camera and LiDAR information are used to understand nearby obstacles, people, and environmental geometry.

4

Planning

Mission and motion planners identify a traversable path toward the selected target location.

5

Decision & Control

Planned motion commands are converted into velocity and heading commands for the quadruped robot.

SPOT Robot Integration and Control

The navigation framework interfaces directly with the SPOT platform to execute autonomous velocity and heading commands. The development environment provides access to robot state, coordinate frames, motion limits, obstacle parameters, and emergency-stop functionality.

This integration allows higher-level autonomous navigation algorithms to operate while retaining the underlying mobility and safety mechanisms available on the quadruped platform.

SPOT robot control and development interface used for configuring motion, monitoring robot state, and integrating autonomous navigation commands.

Localization and 3D Mine Mapping

Reliable localization is essential because underground mines are typically GPS-denied environments. The robot therefore relies primarily on onboard sensing and previously generated spatial information to estimate its pose.

LiDAR observations are accumulated while the robot moves through the mine to construct a detailed representation of tunnels, intersections, chambers, and surrounding structures. The resulting map supports both robot localization and autonomous route planning.

Occupancy Representation

Raw point-cloud measurements are converted into a structured occupancy representation suitable for autonomous navigation. The occupancy map identifies free space, occupied regions, walls, and potential obstacles around the robot.

This representation reduces the complexity of the raw three-dimensional point cloud and allows the path planner to reason directly about traversable and non-traversable regions.

Occupancy-map representation generated from LiDAR data for obstacle-aware navigation through underground corridors and mine structures.

Autonomous Path Planning

The planner uses localization and occupancy information to compute safe trajectories through the mine while maintaining clearance from walls and obstacles. The planned route is continually updated as new sensor observations become available.

Both local navigation and longer-distance mission planning are required. Local planning handles immediate geometry and obstacle avoidance, while the global route guides the robot through extended tunnel networks toward the mission target.

Thermal Perception for Rescue Operations

Visual perception in underground mines can be degraded by darkness, dust, smoke, and other environmental conditions. Thermal imagery therefore provides an additional sensing modality for identifying people when conventional RGB imagery may be unreliable.

The project evaluates deep-learning-based detection using YOLOv11 on a thermal-image dataset. The detector identifies people in different poses and activities, including standing, bending, squatting, and resting positions.

Representative thermal imagery showing people in different postures and environmental conditions relevant to mine safety and rescue scenarios.

Thermal perception results illustrating detection and localization of people in multiple poses using the trained object-detection model.

Thermal Detection Model Evaluation

Multiple object-detection architectures were evaluated on the thermal dataset to assess detection performance and convergence behavior. The comparison includes YOLO-family models and transformer-based detection approaches.

These experiments help identify a suitable perception model for integration with the onboard robotic system, balancing detection accuracy with the computational requirements of field deployment.

Evaluation of thermal-image object detection for mine-rescue perception and onboard deployment.

Evaluation in the Missouri S&T Experimental Mine

The complete robotic system was evaluated in the Missouri University of Science and Technology Experimental Mine, providing a realistic underground environment for testing localization, mapping, navigation, path planning, and perception.

Testing in a real mine environment is important because underground robotics introduces conditions that are difficult to reproduce accurately in laboratory settings. The robot must operate around irregular rock surfaces, narrow tunnels, intersections, uneven ground, changing illumination, and complex three-dimensional structures.

Field validation: the project evaluates the autonomy stack as an integrated physical robotic system rather than only through simulation, combining autonomous navigation, LiDAR-based mapping, obstacle avoidance, and thermal perception in a representative underground mining environment.

Broader Research Impact

Although mine rescue provides the primary motivating application, the underlying autonomy framework is applicable to a broader range of challenging field-robotics scenarios that require reliable navigation without GPS.

Mine Inspection

Routine autonomous inspection of tunnels, infrastructure, and difficult-to-access areas.

Emergency Response

Rapid robotic exploration before deploying human responders into uncertain environments.

Infrastructure Inspection

Navigation and mapping of tunnels, industrial facilities, and other GPS-denied environments.

Physical AI

Integration of multimodal perception, environment understanding, planning, and physical robot execution.

Lightweight Lane-Level Localization with Learned IMU Dead Reckoning

Robust ego-vehicle localization using lightweight lane-level maps, multimodal lane-feature matching, and learning-enhanced inertial dead reckoning without relying on dense 3D point-cloud maps.

Ajay Kumar and collaborators

Project Overview

This work investigates a lightweight localization framework for autonomous vehicles that reduces dependence on large-scale, high-resolution 3D point-cloud maps. Instead, the proposed approach uses abstract lane-level maps and lightweight lane representations to localize the ego vehicle.

Camera and LiDAR measurements are used to extract road and lane features that can be matched against a pre-generated lane-level map. The resulting localization measurements are combined with GNSS, IMU, and vehicle-motion information through probabilistic sensor fusion.

To maintain localization when external observations become unavailable or unreliable, we additionally develop a learning-enhanced IMU dead-reckoning framework. The method combines an invariant Extended Kalman Filter with a neural network that dynamically adapts the inertial-noise parameters used by the filter.

Motivation

Many autonomous-driving localization systems depend on dense LiDAR point-cloud maps containing detailed geometric information about the surrounding environment. Although these maps can provide high localization accuracy, they require significant storage, map-generation effort, and continuous maintenance when the environment changes.

Lane geometry provides a considerably more compact representation of the road network. By matching locally observed lane features to an abstract lane-level map, localization can be performed using a lightweight map representation while preserving information that is highly relevant to vehicle navigation.

Core idea: use lightweight road and lane geometry as the primary map representation, while combining multimodal perception and learned inertial dead reckoning to maintain robust vehicle localization across changing sensor conditions.

Multi-Sensor Localization Framework

The overall localization framework is designed to support multiple pose-estimation sources. Depending on environmental conditions and sensor availability, localization can use GNSS, LiDAR, camera observations, or combinations of these sensing modalities.

Vehicle motion estimated from CAN and IMU measurements provides complementary velocity and orientation information. Pose and motion estimates, together with their uncertainty, are fused to produce a consistent estimate of the vehicle state.

Lane-Level Map Localization

Instead of storing a dense point-cloud representation of every road environment, the proposed localization method relies on a compact lane-level map containing geometric information relevant to autonomous driving.

The local perception system detects lane boundaries and other road structures from the vehicle's camera and LiDAR measurements. These features are transformed into the vehicle coordinate system and associated with corresponding elements in the global lane map.

Lane Feature Extraction and Map Matching

Road features observed from onboard sensors provide the connection between the vehicle and the lane-level map. Camera imagery captures visually distinctive lane boundaries, while LiDAR measurements provide complementary geometric information about road markings and surrounding structure.

The extracted observations are projected into a common coordinate frame and compared with corresponding lane structures in the map. The resulting geometric alignment provides localization corrections while avoiding the storage and matching complexity associated with dense point-cloud maps.

Camera and LiDAR observations used to extract lane geometry and align locally observed road structures with the lightweight lane-level map.

Learning-Enhanced IMU Dead Reckoning

Pure inertial dead reckoning is particularly useful when GNSS, camera, or LiDAR localization becomes temporarily unavailable. However, traditional IMU integration suffers from rapidly accumulating errors caused by sensor noise, bias drift, and uncertainty in the inertial measurement model.

To address this limitation, the proposed method combines an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) with a deep neural network that dynamically adapts the IMU noise parameters used by the filter.

Given accelerometer and gyroscope measurements, the learned noise-parameter adapter predicts the uncertainty parameters required by the state estimator. These parameters are then incorporated into the Kalman-filter update to estimate the vehicle's 3D position, velocity, and orientation while simultaneously estimating IMU biases.

Learning-enhanced inertial dead reckoning combining an AI-based noise-parameter adapter with an invariant Extended Kalman Filter for vehicle-state and uncertainty estimation.

Vehicle Inertial Measurements

The inertial localization module continuously processes three-axis acceleration and angular-rate measurements. Vehicle turns, acceleration, braking, road disturbances, and vibration produce different characteristics in the accelerometer and gyroscope signals.

Learning sensor uncertainty from these motion patterns allows the filter to adapt its confidence in the incoming inertial measurements instead of relying on fixed noise assumptions.

Representative accelerometer and gyroscope measurements collected during vehicle operation and used by the inertial dead-reckoning framework.

KITTI Odometry Evaluation

The learned inertial dead-reckoning framework was evaluated using the KITTI odometry dataset. The experiments examine whether vehicle motion can be reconstructed accurately using IMU measurements while dynamically adapting the uncertainty parameters of the estimator.

The resulting trajectories demonstrate that the inertial framework can estimate vehicle motion over diverse driving sequences and can maintain estimates of position, velocity, orientation, and IMU bias without continuously relying on external localization observations.

Representative trajectory comparisons between estimated vehicle motion and reference trajectories across diverse driving sequences.

Real-Vehicle Experimental Evaluation

The complete localization framework was additionally evaluated across diverse driving conditions to study the behavior of lane-level map matching, inertial dead reckoning, and multi-sensor fusion under realistic vehicle motion.

Different sensor combinations can be selected according to the operating environment. GNSS observations provide global position information when available, while lane-map matching and inertial dead reckoning provide complementary localization capabilities in environments where satellite positioning is degraded or where dense 3D maps are undesirable.

Design objective: provide a flexible localization architecture that can transition between GNSS, lane-feature localization, LiDAR/camera sensing, and learned inertial dead reckoning according to sensor availability and environmental conditions.

Why Lightweight Lane-Level Localization?

Reduced Map Storage

Abstract lane geometry is substantially more compact than storing dense LiDAR point clouds for the complete driving environment.

Easier Map Maintenance

Lane-level representations can be updated more efficiently when road layouts or lane configurations change.

Navigation-Relevant Features

The representation focuses on road and lane structures that directly influence vehicle navigation.

Sensor Flexibility

The architecture supports multiple localization sources rather than depending on a single sensing modality.

GNSS-Degraded Operation

Lane-map matching and inertial propagation provide complementary localization when satellite positioning becomes unreliable.

Learning-Based Adaptation

Dynamic noise estimation allows the inertial filter to adapt to changing vehicle motion and IMU behavior.

Research Impact

The work demonstrates an alternative to localization architectures that rely heavily on dense environmental maps. By combining a lightweight lane-map representation with adaptive inertial estimation, the framework provides a foundation for scalable localization across large road networks.

The underlying concepts can also support autonomous-driving systems that must operate across different localization regimes, including urban roads, highways, GNSS-degraded areas, and environments where maintaining detailed point-cloud maps is impractical.
